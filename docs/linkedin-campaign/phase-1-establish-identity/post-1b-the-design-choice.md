## 1. Post Draft

CADE was not built as a tabletop exercise because tabletop was easy.

I built CADE as a tabletop exercise because the training problem demanded it.

At the time, we did not have much time.

The computer simulation options available to us would have taken time to set up, configure, operate, and explain. They also required English-speaking operators who already understood the system well enough to keep the exercise moving.

That was not the situation we had.

More importantly, those systems were not going to give the training audience the experience they needed most in the time available.

This was the key design question:

What did the staff actually need to practice?

They did not need to learn a software interface.

They did not need a system that tried to model every battlefield variable.

They needed to work through a changing tactical problem, talk through options, make decisions, and see consequences.

That is why CADE took the shape it did.

AI helped me produce the exercise material.

I kept control of the design decisions.

The tabletop format kept the staff focused on judgment instead of software operation.

My boundary statement for CADE is still this:

"CADE fits when the question is staff judgment under pressure. It is not built to model every battlefield variable or replace events designed for system proficiency, command post validation, or certification."

That sentence matters because it keeps the tool honest.

CADE is not trying to be everything.

It is designed for a specific training problem.
