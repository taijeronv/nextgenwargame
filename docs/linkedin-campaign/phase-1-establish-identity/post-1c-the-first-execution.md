## 1. Post Draft

The first real proof was that they did not need me in the room.

The first CADE exercise was run by Task Force Sabre from the Tennessee Army National Guard.

They ran the whole exercise without me having to step in during execution.

That mattered to me.

A product is not proven because the designer can explain it.

It is proven when someone else can pick it up, understand it, run it, and get value from it.

Most of the prep work happened before the exercise.

It was simple by design.

Task Force Sabre did a table read of the situations. They reviewed the turns. They checked that the exercise was aimed at the right training objectives.

Then they ran it.

For the students, this was the first exercise of its type they had experienced.

For the instructor-facilitators, it was also the first exercise of its type they had delivered.

The feedback from both sides was very good.

That was the moment CADE became more than an idea to me.

It transferred.

It did not depend on me being in the room.

It gave a small team a way to deliver useful staff training without needing a heavy simulation environment.

That is still one of the strongest tests I use for AI-assisted work:

Can someone else use the thing successfully when the builder is not there?
