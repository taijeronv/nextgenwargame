## 1. Post Draft

The first design constraint was not the enemy. It was language.

When I started building CADE, I was not designing for an ideal training audience.

I was designing for the audience in front of me.

They were working through interpreters. They were not operating in English as their native language. They also did not come from the same training background that a U.S. military audience would bring into the room.

That changed everything.

I could not write an exercise that depended on dense military language, hidden assumptions, or interpreters having to explain military context they did not have.

That would have been bad design.

It would have put the wrong burden on the interpreters. It would have slowed the students down. It would have made the delivery method part of the problem.

So I had to make a deliberate choice.

I softened the language without softening the military problem.

CADE still had to create pressure. It still had to force staff discussion. It still had to make people decide with incomplete information.

But the friction had to come from the scenario, not from the wording.

That became one of the first rules behind CADE:

Make the problem hard.

Make the language clear.

Do not confuse the two.
