## 1. Campaign Workflow

a. TJ writes the raw post from the outline prompts.

b. The assistant edits for clarity, structure, length, hook strength, and LinkedIn readability.

c. The assistant preserves TJ's substance and phrasing unless the copy is unclear, repetitive, too long, or sounds artificial.

d. The final post should sound like a practitioner writing from lived experience, not a generic AI thought-leadership post.

e. Links do not go in the post body. Links go in the first comment.

f. Each post should address 2–3 questions from the outline. Treat the remaining questions as optional texture or save for a follow-up post. Do not try to answer all questions in a single post.

g. When multiple hooks are listed, choose the one that names the most concrete problem or tension — not the one that sounds most polished. Prefer specificity over abstraction.

## 2. Recommended Cadence

a. Monday: Core campaign post.

b. Wednesday or Thursday: Core campaign post.

c. Friday: Optional lighter post, reflection question, AI workflow note, or practical learning update.

d. Keep at least 48 hours between substantive posts when possible.

e. Start with two core posts per week. Add Friday only when there is a useful, authentic thought to share.

f. Default to text-only posts. Post 3 (Product Evidence) is a candidate for a document post if the CADE decision archive has a visually presentable format. Do not add images unless they carry information — decorative images do not improve performance.

## 3. Target Audience

a. Primary reader: AI integration practitioners and team leads at defense-adjacent companies, government contractors, or technology firms working with military or national security clients. They care about governed AI use and repeatable workflow design.

b. Secondary reader: Military training professionals, program managers, and staff officers exploring AI-assisted exercise design. They understand the constraints TJ worked within and will recognize the CADE problem space immediately.

c. Tertiary reader: Recruiters and hiring managers at organizations building AI-enabled training, simulation, or decision-support products. They are evaluating TJ as a practitioner, not a theorist.

d. Review and update these profiles before Week 1. Adjust hook framing if a specific reader type is the priority for this campaign window.

## 4. Success Criteria

a. Leading indicators (visible within 4 weeks): inbound connection requests from target reader profiles, substantive comments from practitioners rather than generic reactions, direct messages referencing the campaign content.

b. Lagging indicators (visible by Week 8): recruiter or hiring manager outreach, coffee chat or informational interview requests, peer conversations with defense AI or training professionals.

c. A post succeeds if it generates at least one substantive comment or DM from a target reader profile. Likes alone are not a success signal.

d. Evaluate the campaign at the end of Month One. If leading indicators are not present, adjust hook framing before continuing Month Two.

## 5. Engagement Protocol

a. Respond to every substantive comment within the first hour after posting. LinkedIn's algorithm rewards early engagement on new posts.

b. Ask a follow-up question in responses to keep threads alive. Make it specific to what the commenter said — not a generic "what do you think?"

c. Prioritize direct messages from target reader profiles over reacting to generic positive comments.

d. Do not delete or ignore critical comments. Respond with specificity. A thoughtful reply to a challenge is more valuable than a deleted thread.

e. When a post generates an interesting conversation, note the theme. It may become a Friday post or a future core post topic.

## 6. Pre-Launch Profile Checklist

Complete before Week 1 posts go live.

a. Headline reflects current focus — AI integration and training design — not a legacy title or "open to work."

b. About section narrative aligns with the campaign arc: practitioner background, CADE origin, governed AI work.

c. Featured section links to the CADE decision archive or a relevant public artifact.

d. Recent activity section is not empty or stale — post or comment on something before launching the campaign.

e. A brief, non-generic connection request message is ready for inbound requests from target reader profiles.

## 7. Month One Theme

a. Month one establishes identity, origin, and credibility.

b. The audience should understand who TJ is, why CADE matters, and why his AI position comes from real work rather than trend-chasing.

c. The main arc is: practitioner background, planning-first AI work, CADE origin, execution proof, governance position.

## 8. Month Two Theme

a. Month two builds credibility through decisions, lessons learned, and evidence.

b. The audience should see that CADE was not just "AI-generated content." It was a governed product with boundaries, tradeoffs, and repeatable design logic.

c. The main arc is: product evidence, decision records, what AI got wrong, AAR-driven improvement, and collaboration signal.

## 9. Post 0 - Who I Am

a. Purpose: Introduce TJ as a military training practitioner moving into AI development and integration.

b. Reader takeaway: TJ was already exploring AI, but CADE taught him that professional AI work starts with planning, discovery, and human judgment.

c. Hook to use: "I did not discover AI through CADE."

d. Questions for TJ:

What were you doing professionally before CADE?

What kinds of military training problems had you seen before this project?

How were you already exploring AI before CADE?

What changed when you started designing CADE?

What planning or discovery work did you have to do before using AI productively?

What did CADE teach you about the difference between using AI casually and using AI professionally?

What kind of AI work do you want to do next?

e. Do not include: A full CADE explanation, a resume summary, or a direct job pitch.

f. Target length: 1,200 to 1,600 characters.

## 10. Post 1A - The Problem

a. Purpose: Explain the real-world training constraints that shaped CADE.

b. Reader takeaway: CADE began with audience, language, interpreter, time, and training constraints.

c. Hook to use: "The first design constraint was not the enemy. It was language."

d. Questions for TJ:

Who was the training audience?

What made the language environment difficult?

What burden would a conventional exercise have placed on interpreters?

What would have gone wrong if the exercise used dense military language?

How did you decide what to simplify and what not to simplify?

What did this teach you about designing for real users instead of ideal users?

e. Do not include: Technical simulation comparison. Save that for Post 1B.

f. Target length: 1,200 to 1,600 characters.

## 11. Post 1B - The Design Choice

a. Purpose: Explain why CADE became a tabletop exercise instead of a computer simulation.

b. Reader takeaway: Tabletop was not the easy choice. It was the right tool for the training outcome.

c. Hook to use: "CADE was not built as a tabletop exercise because tabletop was easy."

d. Questions for TJ:

What simulation options were available?

Why were they a poor fit for the time available?

What would the training audience have had to learn before using those systems?

What outputs or experiences would those systems not have provided?

What did the staff actually need to practice?

Why did tabletop preserve focus on judgment?

Where did AI help, and where did human design authority stay in control?

e. Do not include: A broad argument against simulations. The point is fit-for-purpose design.

f. Target length: 1,300 to 1,800 characters.

## 12. Post 1C - The First Execution

a. Purpose: Show that CADE worked because another team could run it without the designer in the room.

b. Reader takeaway: The design transferred from creator to operator.

c. Hook to use: "The first real proof was that they did not need me in the room."

d. Questions for TJ:

Who ran the first CADE exercise?

What did Task Force Sabre do before execution?

What did you not have to do during execution?

What feedback did students provide?

What feedback did instructor-facilitators provide?

Why did this prove the design worked?

What does "transferability" mean to you as a product builder?

e. Do not include: Classified details, tactical specifics, or overclaiming impact.

f. Target length: 1,200 to 1,700 characters.

## 13. Post 2 - AI Governance Position

a. Purpose: State TJ's core AI principle: AI as production engine, not design authority.

b. Reader takeaway: TJ uses AI aggressively, but keeps human judgment responsible for decisions, boundaries, and evaluation.

c. Hook options:

"AI can help build the product. It should not become the product owner."

"My strongest AI rule is simple: production engine, not design authority."

"The more I use AI, the more important human judgment becomes."

d. Questions for TJ:

What did AI produce for CADE?

What did you refuse to let AI decide?

What decisions had to stay human-governed?

Where could AI have sounded convincing but been wrong?

Why does governance matter more when AI speeds up production?

How do the CADE decision records support this position?

e. Do not include: Generic AI safety language. Keep it tied to actual product work.

f. Target length: 1,300 to 1,800 characters.

## 14. Post 3 - Product Evidence

a. Purpose: Point readers toward the CADE decision archive as evidence of governed work.

b. Reader takeaway: CADE is not just a claim. There is a public decision record showing how it was designed.

c. Hook options:

"I do not want people to take my AI claims on faith."

"If AI helped build CADE, the design decisions should be inspectable."

"The most important part of CADE may be the decision record behind it."

d. Questions for TJ:

Why did you publish decision records?

What do the records show that a finished product does not show?

What does transparency mean in an AI-assisted project?

How do the records explain what AI produced and what humans governed?

Which decision record best represents your design philosophy?

What should a hiring manager or collaborator learn from the archive?

e. Do not include: Too many links in the body. Put the archive link in the first comment.

f. Target length: 1,100 to 1,600 characters.

## 15. Post 4 - What AI Got Wrong

a. Purpose: Build credibility by explaining where AI failed, misled, overproduced, or required correction.

b. Reader takeaway: TJ is not blindly pro-AI. He understands failure modes from real use.

c. Hook options:

"AI made CADE possible, but it also got important things wrong."

"The fastest way to misuse AI is to trust the first useful-looking answer."

"CADE improved when I stopped treating AI output as finished work."

d. Questions for TJ:

Where did AI produce something plausible but wrong?

Where did it overcomplicate the exercise?

Where did it use language that was too technical, too doctrinal, or too generic?

How did you detect the problem?

What changed in your workflow after that?

What rule would you give someone using AI for serious work?

e. Do not include: Embarrassing vendor/tool callouts unless necessary.

f. Target length: 1,200 to 1,700 characters.

## 16. Post 5 - The AAR As A Design Constraint

a. Purpose: Explain how after-action review requirements shaped CADE design.

b. Reader takeaway: CADE was built not just to run an exercise, but to create useful reflection and learning afterward.

c. Hook options:

"An exercise is only as useful as the conversation it creates afterward."

"I did not design CADE to end at the final turn."

"The AAR was not an add-on. It was a design constraint."

d. Questions for TJ:

What did you need the AAR to reveal?

How did that affect turn design?

What decisions or behaviors did the exercise need to surface?

How did you avoid making the AAR too vague?

What should instructors be able to discuss after the exercise?

How did AI help create material that supported the AAR?

e. Do not include: Generic praise for AARs. Tie it to design decisions.

f. Target length: 1,200 to 1,700 characters.

## 17. Post 6 - What CADE Is Not

a. Purpose: Define CADE's boundaries clearly.

b. Reader takeaway: TJ knows what the product is for and what it is not for.

c. Hook options:

"A useful product needs boundaries."

"CADE is not trying to replace every military training event."

"One reason CADE works is that it does not try to be everything."

d. Questions for TJ:

What should CADE not be used for?

Why is it not a command post validation tool?

Why is it not a system proficiency event?

Why is it not a battlefield simulation?

What is it specifically good at?

Why does naming the boundary make the product stronger?

e. Do not include: Defensive language. Boundaries should sound disciplined, not apologetic.

f. Target length: 1,100 to 1,600 characters.

## 18. Post 7 - The Open Question

a. Purpose: Invite conversation from AI builders, military trainers, defense technologists, and workflow designers.

b. Reader takeaway: TJ is not just broadcasting. He is looking for serious peers and collaborators.

c. Hook options:

"The question I keep coming back to is not whether AI can generate training material."

"I am less interested in AI demos than AI workflows that survive contact with real users."

"The open question for me is where AI belongs in serious training design."

d. Questions for TJ:

What question are you still trying to answer?

What kind of people would you like to hear from?

What kinds of AI workflows are you most interested in?

What have you learned that you want challenged?

Where do you think AI-assisted training design is heading?

What would make a conversation worth having?

e. Do not include: A hard job ask. This should create conversation.

f. Target length: 1,000 to 1,500 characters.

## 19. Post 8 - The Collaboration Signal

a. Purpose: Make it clear what kind of work, teams, and environments TJ wants next.

b. Reader takeaway: TJ wants to work with serious AI advocates in properly resourced development environments.

c. Hook options:

"I want to work with people who take AI seriously enough to govern it."

"The teams I want to join are not asking whether AI is useful. They are asking how to use it responsibly and well."

"I am looking for the kind of AI work where speed and judgment both matter."

d. Questions for TJ:

What kind of team do you want to work with?

What does "properly resourced" mean to you?

What kinds of AI problems are you best suited to help solve?

What do you bring from military training design into AI integration?

What kind of environment would waste your skills?

What kind of conversation should someone start with you?

e. Do not include: "Open to work" language in the body. Keep the public tone confident and collaborative.

f. Target length: 1,000 to 1,500 characters.

## 20. Optional Friday Post Types

a. Friday posts should be lighter, shorter, and more immediate.

b. They do not need to follow the CADE series directly.

c. They should show active learning, practical AI adoption, and a real person behind the campaign.

d. Target length: 500 to 1,000 characters.

e. Post Friday only if you have a genuine observation from that week's actual work. If you are searching for something to say, skip it. A missed Friday is better than a hollow post.

## 21. Friday Idea - Terminal Learning

a. Purpose: Show visible growth from chatbot user to AI power user.

b. Hook options:

"One quiet shift in my AI work: I am spending more time in the terminal."

"Moving from chatbot use to terminal-based workflows changes how you think."

"I used to think the AI interface was the product. I am learning the workflow matters more."

c. Questions for TJ:

What are you learning in the terminal?

What felt intimidating at first?

What can you do now that you could not do before?

How does terminal use change your relationship with AI tools?

What does this say about becoming a stronger AI integrator?

## 22. Friday Idea - AI Stack Note

a. Purpose: Share the tools and workflow habits TJ is using without turning the post into a tool review.

b. Hook options:

"My AI stack is becoming less about tools and more about workflow."

"The best AI tool is the one that fits into a repeatable process."

"I am learning to judge AI tools by what they let me control."

c. Questions for TJ:

What tools are you using regularly?

What role does each tool play?

What changed as you moved from casual use to serious workflow design?

What tool habit has helped the most?

What are you still trying to improve?

## 23. Friday Idea - Reflection Question

a. Purpose: Create lightweight engagement without forcing a pitch.

b. Hook options:

"A question I am sitting with this week:"

"The more I use AI, the more I think about this:"

"Here is the AI workflow question I keep coming back to:"

c. Question examples:

Where should AI speed up the work, and where should it slow the human down?

What part of your workflow should AI never own?

What is the difference between an AI power user and an AI professional?

When does automation become avoidance?

How do you know whether AI improved the work or just produced more of it?

a. Week 1 Monday: Post 0 - Who I Am.

b. Week 1 Wednesday or Thursday: Post 1A - The Problem.

c. Week 1 Friday: Optional terminal learning reflection.

d. Week 2 Monday: Post 1B - The Design Choice.

e. Week 2 Wednesday or Thursday: Post 1C - The First Execution.

f. Week 2 Friday: Optional AI stack note.

g. Week 3 Monday: Post 2 - AI Governance Position.

h. Week 3 Wednesday or Thursday: Post 3 - Product Evidence.

i. Week 3 Friday: Optional reflection question.

j. Week 4 Monday: Post 4 - What AI Got Wrong.

k. Week 4 Wednesday or Thursday: Post 5 - The AAR As A Design Constraint.

l. Week 5 Monday: Post 6 - What CADE Is Not.

m. Week 5 Wednesday or Thursday: Post 7 - The Open Question.

n. Week 6 Monday: Post 8 - The Collaboration Signal.

o. Week 6 Wednesday or Thursday: Decision record post — select one record from the CADE archive that best illustrates a design tradeoff. Purpose: show the reasoning behind a specific choice, not just the outcome.

p. Week 7 Monday: AI workflow post — describe a current terminal or workflow practice in concrete terms. This is a practical Friday-style post elevated to a core slot if the workflow is substantive enough.

q. Week 7 Wednesday or Thursday: Response post — if Posts 7 or 8 generated a meaningful conversation thread, write a follow-up that advances the most interesting question that emerged. If no clear thread exists, run a second decision record post.

r. Week 8: Use remaining slots for content driven by actual conversations from Weeks 1–7. Prioritize topics where readers engaged specifically. If no organic direction has emerged, run a second governance post or a lessons-learned post from Month One.

## 25. Editing Checklist

a. The first line must stand alone as a hook.

b. The post should make one main point.

c. The post should sound like TJ, not like a brand account.

d. The post should include concrete details from lived experience.

e. The post should avoid inflated claims.

f. The post should avoid generic AI phrases such as "unlock potential," "revolutionize," "game changer," and "harness the power."

g. The post should end with a clear thought, not a forced engagement question unless the post is explicitly designed as a question post.

h. The post body should not include external links.

i. Does this post need a call to action? If yes, is it specific (connect, reply, DM) or implicit? Posts designed to generate conversation should signal what kind of response is wanted. Posts that make a strong point can end without a CTA. Do not force an engagement question onto a post that is not designed for it.
